Question 1

Briefly describe what this function check_model() does and why it might be more efficient than running individual tests manually.

This function runs a comprehensive set of model assumption checks all at once, producing a visual diagnostic output in a single call.

From the documentation, it consolidates several individual checks that the package offers separately, including check_collinearity(), check_normality(), and check_heteroscedasticity(), among others. Rather than calling each of these one by one, check_model() bundles them into one unified visualization.

The practical advantage is straightforward; instead of writing and running multiple lines of diagnostic code, interpreting each result in isolation, and mentally piecing together an overall picture of model health, you get everything surfaced at once. This is especially useful during exploratory modeling, where you want a quick sanity check on assumptions before committing to a model. It reduces the cognitive overhead of juggling multiple outputs and makes it easier to spot if several assumptions are violated simultaneously, which might point to a more fundamental modeling issue rather than an isolated one.

Posterior Predictive Check

The posterior predictive check reveals an immediate and fundamental concern with this model. The observed data (dark green line) displays a clearly bimodal distribution, with one peak around a blood glucose level of approximately 80 and a second, taller peak around 150-160. The model-predicted lines fail to replicate this pattern entirely, instead generating a single smooth unimodal hump centered around the higher peak. This tells us that the model is assuming the outcome comes from one homogeneous population, when the data clearly suggests there are two distinct subgroups present. This is arguably the most serious finding in this diagnostic report, as it indicates a fundamental mismatch between what the model assumes about the data and what the data actually looks like.

Linearity

The linearity plot shows residuals on the y-axis plotted against fitted values on the x-axis, with a green reference line that should ideally sit flat at zero across the entire range. Here, the fitted values span a narrow range from approximately 133 to 150. The reference line starts relatively flat between fitted values of 133 and 140, but then curves noticeably upward after 145, swinging toward the upper right of the plot. This upward curl indicates that as predicted values increase, the model systematically underestimates the outcome. A truly linear relationship would produce a reference line that stays horizontal throughout, so this deviation tells us the underlying relationship has some curvature that a straight line cannot adequately capture.

Homogeneity of Variance

The homogeneity of variance plot, also known as the scale-location plot, displays the square root of standardized residuals against fitted values. If the assumption of constant variance (homoskedasticity) held, the green reference line would be flat and the dots would form an even horizontal band. Instead, the reference line begins around 0.85-0.90 on the left side and climbs steadily to approximately 1.1-1.15 by the right side of the plot. While this is not a dramatic spike, the consistent upward trend across the entire range of fitted values is meaningful. It indicates that the model’s errors grow larger as predicted values increase, a violation known as heteroskedasticity. The practical consequence of this is that the standard errors produced by the model are not equally reliable across all predicted values, which in turn means that p-values and confidence intervals, particularly at the higher end, should be treated with caution.

Influential Observations

The vast majority of data points are clustered tightly near zero leverage with standardized residuals comfortably between -2 and +2. A handful of labelled observations (821, 397, 350, 225) show slightly elevated leverage around 0.003-0.005, but their standardized residuals remain close to zero, meaning they are not badly mispredicted. The point with the highest leverage, observation 802 at approximately 0.032, similarly has a residual near zero. Crucially, all observations fall well within the Cook’s distance contour lines, which represent the threshold beyond which a point would be considered disproportionately influential. This assumption is satisfactorily met: no single data point is tilting the regression line in a meaningful way.

Normality of Residuals

This plot displays deviations from expected normal quantiles rather than the raw quantiles themselves, which is why it looks different from a classic QQ plot. Ideally the dots would track closely along the horizontal green line at zero throughout. What we observe instead is that on the far left (around x = -2.5 to -2), the dots start high at around +1.5 to +2.1 before dropping steeply. Through the middle section the dots follow a wavy, staircase-like pattern rather than a smooth curve, hovering near but not on zero. On the right side there is a sharp upward jump to around +1 to +1.5 before the final point drops back down. The jagged staircase pattern through the middle is characteristic of an outcome variable that is discrete or heavily rounded, meaning the residuals only take on certain values rather than varying continuously. The sharp deviations at both tails indicate the residual distribution is not normal. This finding is consistent with the bimodal structure already observed in the posterior predictive check.

  • Based on the visual evidence across these five diagnostic plots, this model is not safe to interpret in its current form. Four out of five assumptions show meaningful violations, and they all point toward the same underlying problem.

  • The posterior predictive check is the clearest signal: the model cannot reproduce the shape of the actual data because it is blind to what appears to be two distinct subgroups within the sample. This may mean the model is fundamentally misrepresenting the population it is trying to describe.

SECTION 3: bootstrapping

QUESTION 8

Explain in what circumstances using bootstrapped confidence intervals can be better than what we’ve done so far.

The most obvious case is the linear model predicting blood glucose from BMI. The diagnostic plots clearly showed the residuals were non-normal and the outcome was bimodal — two distinct subgroups present in the data. Every confidence interval and p-value that model produced was computed assuming normally distributed errors, which the diagnostics proved wasn’t the case. Bootstrap doesn’t make that assumption, it works with the actual shape of your data, so the uncertainty estimates it produces would have been more honest.

On the logistic model specifically, bootstrap can help in a meaningful way. The binned residuals plot showed systematic misprediction at low predicted probabilities, which hints that the model is struggling in that region. The standard confidence intervals around our coefficients and odds ratios assume the model is correctly specified and that the sampling distribution of the estimates is approximately normal. Bootstrap doesn’t assume either of those things, it would ask “if we resampled this dataset repeatedly, how much would our estimates move around?” That gives you a more honest picture of uncertainty, particularly in the low probability region where the model was already showing signs of strain.

(Potentially) The confidence intervals we reported for the average marginal effect of BMI were still computed under standard distributional assumptions. A bootstrap confidence interval around that same estimate would allow for a more meaningful statement to a health agency audience, instead of reporting a p-value, we could say “there is only a 2.5% chance the true effect is zero or negative”, which is the kind of plain probability statement that actually informs real decisions rather than hiding behind statistical jargon that most people misinterpret anyway.

library(tidyverse)

# STEP 1: Create an empty "bucket" (vector) to hold our results
# We will do this 500 times
n_iterations <- 500
glucose_estimates <- numeric(n_iterations)

# STEP 2: The Loop (The "Manual" work)
for (i in 1:n_iterations) {
  
  # A. Create a "Resample" (Pick rows at random with replacement)
  boot_data <- diabetes[sample(nrow(diabetes), replace = TRUE), ]
  
  # B. Run the model on THIS specific simulated dataset
  boot_model <- glm(diabetes ~ blood_glucose_level + age, 
                    family = "binomial", data = boot_data)
  
  # C. Save the Glucose coefficient into our "bucket" at position i
  glucose_estimates[i] <- coef(boot_model)["blood_glucose_level"]
  
  # For pedagogu: Print progress every 100 times so students see it working
  if(i %% 100 == 0) print(paste("Simulation number", i, "complete!"))
}

The bootstrap distribution shows the estimated effect of smoking history on heart disease probability across 500 resamples of the data. Both the current smoker (pink) and ex-smoker (teal) distributions are centered around zero and straddle it in both directions, meaning the effect is inconsistent across resamples with no clear positive or negative signal. This fails to support the hypothesis that smokers have a higher probability of heart disease than non-smokers.

The current smoker distribution additionally shows extreme outliers at both ends of the x-axis, reflecting instability caused by the small number of current smokers in the sample (n=105). In some resamples, near-zero heart disease cases in that group caused the model to produce unreliable estimates. This instability is itself a finding: it suggests the sample size is insufficient to draw reliable conclusions about current smokers specifically, and that a larger dataset would be needed to properly test this hypothesis.
